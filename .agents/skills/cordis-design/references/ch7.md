# 7. Related Work

## Summary

Chapter 7 positions Cordis against four bodies of prior work — effect and coeffect systems,
programming paradigms, temporal composability, and spatial composability — to make precise
what is genuinely new in the paper's contribution versus what it inherits or resembles. The
chapter's method throughout is comparative and axis-based: for each related line, it names the
shared premise and then isolates the one or two structural axes on which Cordis differs (purpose,
setting, scope, reach, vocabulary, or lifecycle integration), rather than claiming wholesale novelty.
Section 7.1 shows that monadic effect systems, capability-based algebraic effects, reversible-effect
calculi, and graded-type unifications of effects/coeffects each anticipate a piece of Cordis's
theory (tracking, capability framing, invertibility, or the effect/coeffect duality) but stop short of
runtime, dynamically-recomposable tracking. Section 7.2 contrasts the context paradigm with
purely functional state-threading and imperative implicit mutation, then with context-oriented
programming (COP) and aspect-oriented programming (AOP), arguing Cordis keeps their
ergonomic or crosscutting benefits while adding structural tracking and reversal that those
paradigms lack. Section 7.3 surveys temporal composability — stateful forward migration (DSU,
Erlang/OTP, HMR), developer-authored recovery (plugin lifecycles, Command pattern, sagas,
React's `useEffect`), statically scoped reversal (STM, reversible computing, linear types/RAII),
and interposed reclamation (Nooks, shadow drivers, Akeso) — establishing that Cordis's
revertible-effect invariant is the only mechanism among these that requires no hand-written
migration code, imposes no fixed lexical scope, and generalizes to full component unloading.
Section 7.4 opens the spatial-composability survey but is cut off by the page range. This chapter
matters because it is where the paper defends its claim to novelty by close, structural comparison
rather than by assertion, and because it supplies the vocabulary (quiescence, tranquility, DSU,
COP layers, AOP pointcuts/advice, causal-consistency) an implementer needs to map Cordis's
guarantees onto familiar systems concepts.

## Section-by-Section

### 7.1. Effect and Coeffect Systems

- The section opens by noting Section 2 already reviewed effects/coeffects as Cordis's theoretical pillars, so 7.1 instead surveys three research lines that extend them: capabilities, reversibility, and unified grading, after first situating industrial monadic effect systems.
- Monadic effect systems (ZIO's `ZIO[R,E,A]`, Effect-TS's `Effect<A,E,R>`, fp-ts's Reader-based monad transformers [84–86]) encode effects as monadic values with typed result/error/requirement channels, but differ from Cordis on two axes: (1) tracking requires writing the program inside the effect type (a monadic embedding), whereas Cordis overlays tracking on ordinary host code; (2) a requirement is discharged by an installed service's interpretation, and withdrawing that service leaves its past effects in place, whereas Cordis pairs every effect with an inverse and re-resolves requirements as providers come and go (citing Sections 3.1 and 3.2).
- Algebraic effects as capabilities: Brachthäuser et al.'s Effekt language [87, 88] reinterprets effect types as capabilities — what a computation requires from context rather than what it produces — which the chapter says shares Cordis's view of context as a capability mediator, but differs (1) in purpose (Effekt enables modular interpretation/multiple handler semantics; Cordis enables tracking and reversion by pairing transformations with inverses) and (2) in setting (Effekt disciplines effects statically at the type level with second-class, lexically-scoped capabilities by default, recoverable to first-class via "boxing"; Cordis disciplines effects at runtime aiming at complete resource recovery on component removal, with Section 6.7 addressing what a second-class-context language variant would offer).
- Reversible effect semantics: Heunen et al. [89] adapt Hughes' arrows into dagger arrows and inverse arrows in a denotational, categorical setting, modeling effects like serialization and mutable store as invertible by construction — the formal account closest to Cordis's revertible effects, since both pair each effect with an undo mechanism rather than a handler.
- The key distinction from Heunen et al. is the demand placed on reversibility: their setting requires the whole computation be globally, two-sidedly invertible (guaranteed categorically), while Cordis requires only that each atomic effect admit a one-sided inverse supplied by the caller at the point of application, from which composite inverses follow by composition (Section 3.1) — a strictly weaker and more locally satisfiable requirement.
- Graded types as unified effects/coeffects: Orchard et al. [90] proposed graded modal types unifying graded monads (effects) and graded comonads (coeffects) in the Granule language, later extended to imperative Java-like languages [91, 92] and call-by-push-value [93]; all of this work is static, checking annotations at compile time over lexically fixed scopes.
- The chapter frames Cordis's contribution as orthogonal rather than competing: it lifts the same effect/coeffect duality to runtime mechanisms so that temporal retraction and spatial dependency are re-resolved as the set of loaded components evolves, instead of being settled once over fixed program text.

### 7.2. Programming Paradigms

- The context paradigm (Section 3.3) mediates every effect and coeffect through an explicit context; 7.2 first contrasts it with functional/imperative treatments of side effects, then with COP and AOP.
- Explicit threading vs. implicit mutation: the State monad `S → (A, S)` [23] threads environment explicitly through every computation (securing equational reasoning but forcing every function on the call path to accept/return state regardless of use), while imperative languages leave effects and dependencies implicit at the call site, making a call's system-wide impact legible only by transitively reading its implementation, so moving or removing a call can silently break distant invariants.
- The context paradigm is positioned as combining the traceability of explicit threading with the ergonomics of implicit mutation: effects/coeffects pass through a context the component holds, so every operation is attributable to its invoking context (and hence component), while everything else remains ordinary host code — an overlay realizable atop either functional or imperative host languages, fixing each operation's denotation while leaving its realization "in place" where the host mutates and "derived" where it stays pure (Definition 23).
- Context-oriented programming (COP) [94–96] equips a language with layers — partial method/class definitions activated and deactivated at runtime by execution context — so behavior adapts without base code naming its context dependencies; COP and Cordis coincide only nominally in treating context as first-class and runtime-mutable and in dynamically activating/deactivating behavior.
- The substantive difference: in COP, "context" is the ambient execution situation (location, user, mode) and activation changes method dispatch within a dynamically scoped extent, with layers neither tracking nor reverting the side effects they induce, and activation ungoverned by dependency satisfaction; in Cordis, context is the Γ∞ entity mediating effects/coeffects, activation runs a component's revertible effects driven by reactive coeffect satisfaction (Section 3.2), and deactivation reverts them in full — a trade-off where COP gains dynamically-scoped layer extents at the cost of language specificity, while Cordis resolves activation reactively as a language-agnostic overlay, able to express only COP's "global, value-driven fragment" (context-dependent implementation selection) but not dynamically-scoped activation.
- Aspect-oriented programming (AOP) [97, 98] modularizes a crosscutting concern into an aspect: a pointcut quantifying over join points in the base program plus advice woven in at each; Cordis's analogue of an aspect is a coeffect — a shared mediation point multiple components declare dependence on, letting crosscutting behavior be reshaped there without editing any component.
- The two paradigms differ on two axes: (1) declaration vs. obliviousness — an AOP pointcut is oblivious and quantified over arbitrary join points whose code is unaware it is advised, whereas Cordis confines crosscutting to explicitly declared coeffects, giving determinacy and traceability (an orchestrator can inspect/govern what cross-cuts a component at the configuration layer without reading its source, unlike an AOP concern legible only through its aspects); (2) lifecycle integration — a Cordis crosscutting change is carried by a component's effects, reverted on unload and propagated reactively to dependents as one move within the dynamic composition model, whereas dynamic-AOP systems [99, 100] weave/unweave at runtime as a standalone operation neither bound to component lifecycle nor triggering re-resolution among advised code.

### 7.3. Temporal Composability

- Temporal composability concerns replacing/removing a running component while recovering the effects it installed; the chapter divides prior approaches into four families by how they treat a departing component's state and effects.
- Stateful forward migration: all such systems require a component be swapped only at a safe, interaction-free point — Kramer and Magee's quiescence [53], later relaxed by Vandewoude et al. to tranquility [54]; Cordis's rolling-update pattern (Section 6.2) enforces this by draining in-flight requests before unloading a provider.
- Dynamic software updating (DSU) migrates state forward via hand-written transformation functions — Hicks et al.'s general-purpose DSU for C [101], Stoyle et al.'s type-safe update points via con-freeness analysis [102], Hayden et al.'s Kitsune [103] — mapping old-version data to new-version representations and inheriting heap objects, open files, and connections in place; Overeem et al. [104] apply the same discipline to persistent event-store schema migration, and Erlang/OTP [15] migrates process state via `code_change/3` while recovering from faults by restarting supervised processes; JavaScript HMR (webpack [48], Vite [49]) hands state forward through `module.hot`/`import.meta.hot` at the module level.
- Compared with Cordis's module replacement (Section 5.2), these DSU/HMR approaches migrate in-memory state more gracefully (Cordis reverts the old component's tracked effects and reapplies the new component's from a clean slate, so in-memory state does not survive a reload unless placed in a longer-lived dependency, with layering DSU-style forward migration atop revertible effects left as future work); Cordis is nonetheless more general because it needs no hand-written migration functions and supports unloading a component entirely with full resource recovery, not merely in-place updating.
- Developer-authored recovery: plugin lifecycle conventions (OSGi [52], Eclipse extension points, IntelliJ, VSCode), the Command pattern's undo method [105], the saga model's compensating actions per step [51], algebraic effect handler finalizers [106], and event sourcing's compensating events [107] all treat the inverse as an unenforced duty decoupled from the operation, so a forgotten inverse leaks resources silently (as documented empirically in Section 1.2.1).
- React's `useEffect` [108] comes closest to structurally pairing an effect with its inverse (a returned cleanup the runtime invokes before re-execution and on unmount), but its composability is limited: a hook may be called only at a component's or another hook's top level, never in a conditional/loop/nested function, and its effect body accepts neither an async function nor an iterator — so effects cannot be assembled from other effects or interleaved with control flow, leaving nothing from which a composite inverse could be derived; Cordis effects carry no such restriction, compose freely, may run asynchronously, and require a hand-written inverse only per atomic effect, with composite inverses derived by composition, making complete recovery an invariant of the system rather than a matter of developer discipline.
- Statically scoped reversal: software transactional memory [109, 110] (descended from hardware transactional memory [111]) logs reads/writes so a group of memory operations commits or aborts atomically; reversible computing (Landauer and Bennett [112, 113], languages like Janus [114]) makes every step of a whole computation globally invertible; reversible process calculi build backtracking into the semantics itself — RCCS [115] carries a memory per process and allows taking back a step when the resulting past is causally equivalent, and Phillips and Ulidowski [116] derive reversible operators uniformly for CCS, ACP, and CSP while preserving forward operational semantics.
- The chapter maps RCCS/Phillips-Ulidowski's causal-consistency criterion to the concurrent counterpart of Cordis's own recovery order — an accumulator applying a component's inverses in last-in-first-out order, with the guard of Section 4.2.2 deferring a provider's withdrawal until its consumers have deactivated (Theorem 70) — but notes their reach is fixed by the semantics (every action performed remains undoable), whereas a Cordis component supplies an inverse per atomic effect and its accumulator brings the context back only to where its own composition began.
- Linear types [117], RAII [4], and Rust's ownership system [63] tie resource release to a lexical region, fixing scope and reach of reversal statically; Cordis fixes no such scope in advance, reverting arbitrary context operations over a component's lifecycle and treating lexical resource management as complementary for local, single-component resources.
- Kim and Rinard [118] specify and verify an inverse for every state-changing operation of set/map collection implementations, together with commutativity conditions reasoned over abstract state (so equivalent-but-not-identical resulting structures count as commuting); the chapter notes both ingredients Section 3.4 requires of a coeffect are mechanically checkable this way, and their stated reason for preferring an inverse over a saved state copy is the same one Section 3.1 acts on — but their inverses do not compose (each verified per single operation), whereas a Cordis accumulator carries the inverses of a whole lifecycle.
- Interposed reclamation: Nooks [79] wraps every call across the Linux kernel/loadable-extension boundary through an object tracker recording what a recovery manager should release on extension failure; shadow drivers [80] tap the same calls from the other side to record state for restoring a restarted driver instance; Akeso [119] uses compiler instrumentation to divide kernel execution into nestable recovery domains logging state changes and cross-thread dependencies, rolling back a faulting request together with every dependent domain.
- This family is called the closest systems-level precedent for revertible effects because reclamation follows from a runtime-maintained record rather than developer-remembered cleanup, but it differs from Cordis in vocabulary and reach: the platform fixes what can be recorded (release code per kernel object type, one shadow per driver class, an inverse per instrumented allocator), so a component may hold only resources the platform already knows how to release, whereas a Cordis component introduces its own effects with a per-atomic-effect inverse (Section 3.1); reclamation is also bounded to a single committing request or restart of the same extension, whereas Cordis reverts over a component's whole lifetime and propagates removal to dependents, which release their own effects in turn (Section 3.2).

### 7.4. Spatial Composability

- Spatial composability concerns how a component's dependencies on others are declared and bound; the chapter states prior mechanisms divide by how binding responds to change: wiring dependencies once at initialization, reacting to whole-component availability, or propagating change at the granularity of individual values.

## Terminology

| Term | Notation | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| Monadic effect system | `ZIO[R,E,A]`, `Effect<A,E,R>` | A type-system encoding of effects as monadic values (result, typed error, and context-requirement parameters) that a runtime executes by interpreting the effect type; contrasted with Cordis's overlay-on-host-code approach. |
| Algebraic effects as capabilities | — | Brachthäuser et al.'s Effekt reinterpretation of effect types as expressing what a computation requires from its context (a capability) rather than what side effects it produces. |
| Boxing (Effekt) | — | The mechanism in Effekt that lifts the default restriction of second-class, lexically-scoped capabilities to recover first-class use, by tracking captured capabilities in types. |
| Dagger arrows / inverse arrows | — | Heunen et al.'s categorical adaptation of Hughes' arrows that models reversible side effects (e.g., serialization, mutable store) as invertible morphisms in a denotational setting. |
| One-sided inverse | — | Cordis's weaker reversibility requirement: each atomic effect need only admit an inverse supplied by the caller at the point of application, from which composite inverses follow by composition, rather than requiring global two-sided invertibility. |
| Graded modal types | — | Orchard et al.'s unifying type-theoretic notion covering both graded monads (effects) and graded comonads (coeffects) within a single static type system, realized in the Granule language. |
| Context paradigm | Γ∞ | Cordis's paradigm in which every effect and coeffect is mediated through an explicit, runtime-mutable context entity (Γ∞), combining the traceability of explicit state-threading with the ergonomics of implicit mutation. |
| State monad | `S → (A, S)` | The classical functional-programming construct that threads an environment/state value explicitly through every computation in the call path, securing equational reasoning at the cost of universal threading. |
| Context-oriented programming (COP) | — | A paradigm equipping a language with "layers" (partial method/class definitions) that activate and deactivate at runtime according to the ambient execution context (location, user, mode), changing method dispatch without base code naming its dependencies. |
| Layer (COP) | — | A COP unit of partial behavior redefinition that is activated/deactivated dynamically; unlike a Cordis component, a layer neither tracks nor reverts the side effects its activation induces. |
| Aspect-oriented programming (AOP) | — | A paradigm that modularizes a crosscutting concern into an aspect, consisting of a pointcut (a quantification over join points in the base program) and advice (code woven in at each matched join point). |
| Pointcut / advice | — | AOP's mechanism for obliviously selecting arbitrary join points in code unaware it is being advised, and inserting behavior there; contrasted with Cordis's declared-coeffect crosscutting. |
| Quiescence | — | Kramer and Magee's criterion that a running component may be replaced only once it reaches a safe, interaction-free point. |
| Tranquility | — | Vandewoude et al.'s relaxation of quiescence into a less disruptive timing criterion for safely replacing a running component. |
| Dynamic software updating (DSU) | — | A family of techniques (e.g., for C, or via con-freeness analysis, or Kitsune) that migrates a running program's state forward across code versions using hand-written transformation functions, inheriting heap objects, files, and connections in place. |
| Con-freeness analysis | — | Stoyle et al.'s type-safety analysis used to identify safe update points for dynamic software updating. |
| `code_change/3` | `code_change/3` | The Erlang/OTP callback used to migrate a supervised process's state forward during a code upgrade, paired with restart-based fault recovery rather than effect reversion. |
| Hot Module Replacement (HMR) | `module.hot`, `import.meta.hot` | A JavaScript build-tool mechanism (webpack, Vite) that hands a module's state forward across a reload via a dedicated hot-reload API, analogous to but less general than Cordis's module replacement. |
| Command pattern | — | A design pattern that encapsulates an operation together with an explicit undo method, used to build undo/redo stacks; the inverse is developer-authored and decoupled from the operation. |
| Saga model | — | A pattern structuring a long-lived distributed transaction as a sequence of steps, each paired with a hand-written compensating action to undo it if a later step fails. |
| Event sourcing | — | A persistence pattern that retracts prior state not by executing an inverse operation but by appending new compensating events to the event log. |
| `useEffect` | `useEffect` | React's hook API pairing an effect with an optional returned cleanup function the runtime invokes before re-execution and on unmount; limited to top-level hook calls and synchronous, non-iterator effect bodies, which blocks composing effects or deriving composite inverses. |
| Software transactional memory (STM) | — | A concurrency-control technique that logs reads/writes to memory so a group of operations either commits atomically or aborts, rolling memory back to its pre-transaction state. |
| Reversible computing | — | A model of computation, tracing to Landauer and Bennett's thermodynamic analyses and realized in languages like Janus, in which every step of a whole computation is globally invertible. |
| RCCS | — | Reversible CCS: a process calculus in which each process carries a memory of its past, and a step may be taken back when the resulting earlier state is causally equivalent to one previously reached. |
| Causal-consistency criterion | — | The property, derived uniformly for CCS/ACP/CSP by Phillips and Ulidowski, governing when a reversible step may be taken back; the chapter treats it as the concurrent counterpart of Cordis's own LIFO recovery order. |
| Accumulator (Cordis) | — | The runtime structure that records a component's applied inverses and replays them in last-in-first-out order to revert the component's effects on removal. |
| Theorem 70 | — | Cited (not restated in this chapter) as establishing that the Section 4.2.2 guard defers a provider's withdrawal until its consumers have deactivated. |
| Linear types / RAII / ownership | — | Static disciplines (linear type systems, C++ RAII, Rust's ownership system) that tie a resource's release to a lexically fixed scope or region, contrasted with Cordis's non-lexically-scoped reversion over a component's whole lifecycle. |
| Interposed reclamation | — | A family of systems-level techniques (Nooks, shadow drivers, Akeso) that reclaim resources a component acquired by recording its acquisitions at a runtime-controlled interface, rather than relying on the component to supply inverses itself. |

## Relevance to an Agent Harness

- The developer-authored-recovery critique (plugin lifecycle conventions like OSGi, Eclipse, IntelliJ, VSCode unload callbacks) directly warns a plugin-harness builder: unload callbacks written by hand are an unenforced duty, and a forgotten one leaks resources silently — exactly the failure mode a harness should design out via structural effect/inverse pairing rather than convention.
- The `useEffect` composability critique is a concrete design lesson for any plugin/module system offering lifecycle hooks: restricting hook placement (top-level only, no conditionals/loops) and disallowing async/iterator effect bodies blocks composing effects from sub-effects, which in turn blocks deriving a composite inverse for hot-unload — a harness's plugin API should avoid this restriction if it wants composable teardown.
- The DSU/HMR comparison implies a concrete trade-off for hot-module-replacement style reload in an agent harness: full state-migration (à la webpack/Vite `module.hot`) is more graceful for preserving in-memory state, but Cordis's clean-slate revert-and-reapply approach needs no hand-written migration function and, notably, is the only approach here that also supports full unloading (not just in-place update) with complete resource recovery — a harness wanting general plugin unload/hot-swap should prefer this trade-off unless it specifically needs seamless in-memory continuity.
- The quiescence/tranquility discussion gives a concrete timing rule for safe hot-swap: a component (plugin) should only be swapped at a safe, interaction-free point, implemented in Cordis's own system by draining in-flight requests before unloading a provider — directly applicable to any plugin harness's rolling-update or hot-reload sequencing.
- The interposed-reclamation family (Nooks, shadow drivers, Akeso) is the closest systems-level analogy for sandboxing/isolation: wrapping every call across a trust boundary (e.g., kernel/extension, or harness/plugin) through a tracked interface lets a supervisor reclaim exactly what a failed or unloaded component acquired, but the chapter notes this approach's resources are limited to whatever the platform already knows how to track, unlike a scheme (Cordis's) where the component itself supplies inverses for its own effects — a harness combining both (platform-level tracking of coarse resources plus plugin-declared inverses for custom ones) would cover more ground.
- The AOP/coeffect comparison bears on dependency injection and config reloading: Cordis's design goal — that crosscutting behavior be confined to explicitly declared coeffects rather than obliviously matched by quantified pointcuts — supports building a harness where an orchestrator can inspect and govern what cross-cuts a plugin purely from configuration, without reading or analyzing the plugin's source, improving auditability of injected dependencies.
- The COP contrast underscores a limitation relevant to config-driven behavior switching: a Cordis-style reactive-coeffect system can express context-dependent implementation selection (the "global, value-driven" case) but not COP's dynamically-scoped layer activation — so a harness needing scoped, temporary behavior overrides (not just global swaps) may need mechanisms beyond what this paradigm offers.

## Open Questions and Limitations

- The chapter explicitly defers "layering DSU-style forward migration atop revertible effects" (carrying in-memory state forward across a reload rather than reverting-and-reapplying from a clean slate) as future work.
- Section 6.7, referenced but not included in this extracted text, is flagged as the place that addresses "what a language that made the context second class in this sense would offer" — i.e., the trade-offs of a statically-scoped (Effekt-like) alternative to Cordis's runtime discipline are left to that section rather than resolved in Chapter 7.
- The extracted chapter text cuts off mid-way through Section 7.4 (Spatial Composability), after only the framing sentence dividing prior mechanisms into three binding-update strategies (once at initialization, whole-component availability, or individual-value propagation); no specific citations, comparisons, or conclusions for spatial composability are present in the provided pages (77–81), so this section's content cannot be summarized beyond that framing.
- Several claims rely on theorems/definitions stated elsewhere in the paper (Theorem 70, Definition 23, Sections 3.1/3.2/3.4/4.2.2/5.2/6.1/6.2) that this chapter cites but does not restate, so their exact formal content is not verifiable from this chapter alone.
- The chapter itself frames its contribution in Section 7.1 as "orthogonal" to the static graded-type literature rather than as a strict improvement, implicitly acknowledging that static compile-time effect/coeffect checking (which Cordis forgoes in favor of runtime tracking) offers guarantees runtime tracking does not attempt to replicate.
